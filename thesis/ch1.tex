\section{Physics of skyrmions}

Multiple distinct mechanisms can result in the emergence of magnetic skyrmions \cite{fert2017magnetic}. In some cases, skyrmions can emerge solely due to dipole interactions, but typically they are facilitated through an antisymmetric exchange. The consequences of such interaction were first observed as weak ferromagnetism in antiferromagnetic crystals in the early 20th century. A theoretical understanding of this phenomenon was provided by \emph{Igor Dzyaloshinskii} \cite{dzyaloshinsky1958thermodynamic}, who showed it is caused by relativistic spin-lattice and magnetic dipole interaction. \emph{Toru Moriya} \cite{PhysRev.120.91} identified the spin-orbit coupling as the microscopic mechanism behind this interaction. Acknowledging their work, the antisymmetric exchange is referred to as \emph{Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya (DM) interaction}.

\subsection{Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction}

Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction is essential in forming magnetic skyrmions and magnetic spirals in chiral magnets. It is a magnetic exchange interaction between particles with non-zero magnetic moments, characterized by the Hamiltonian:

\begin{equation}
	\label{DM-interaction-hamiltonian}
	H^{\text{DM}} = \sum_{ij} \bm{D}_{ij} \dot (\bm{S}_i \times \bm{S}_j),
\end{equation}

where $\bm{D}_{ij}$ is a DM vector between the $i$-th and $j$-th spin, and $\bm{S}_k$ is a unit vector in the direction of the magnetic moment, i.e. $\bm{S}_k = \nicefrac{\bm{\mu_k}}{\lVert \bm{\mu_k} \rVert}$. The Hamiltonian shows that the DM interaction prefers the perpendicular orientation of neighboring spins. The direction of DM vector $\bm{D}_{ij}$ is given by the symmetry of the system, and that in turn influences the topology of skyrmions and spirals.

The full Hamiltonian also contains two other terms: spin-to-spin exchange interaction and the effect of the external magnetic field. The spin-to-spin exchange interaction is represented by:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:exchange_interaction_hamiltonian}
	H^\text{exchange} = - \sum_{ij} J_{ij} \bm{S}_i \cdot \bm{S}_j,
\end{equation}

where $J_{ij}$ is the exchange integral which depends on the charge density overlap between the $i$-th and $j$-th spin \cite{kittel2018introduction}. Lastly, the effect of a homogeneous magnetic field is represented by.

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:exchange_interaction}
	H^\text{B} = - \sum_{i} \bm{B} \cdot \bm{S}_i,
\end{equation}

The full Hamiltonian is therefore: 

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:full-hamiltonian}
	H = - \sum_{ij} J_{ij} \bm{S}_i \cdot \bm{S}_j
	- \sum_{ij} \bm{D}_{ij} \cdot \left[\bm{S}_i \times \bm{S}_j\right] 
	- \bm{B} \sum_i S_i,
\end{equation}

\section{Data generation}

The dataset we use in this thesis consists of magnetic configurations on a square lattice. The configurations were simulated with the \emph{Uppsala Atomic Spin Dynamics package (UppASD)} \cite{skubic2008method, eriksson2017atomistic}. The final state is reached via \emph{simulated annealing}, where we start with a random spin configuration at an initial temperature, and iteratively propose random spin updates, while lowering the temperature. At higher temperatures, we accept energy-increasing updates with some probability. However, this probability will decrease with the lowering of temperature, and we start accepting only those configurations that reduce the Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian used in the simulation is further simplified, compared to \ref{eq:full-hamiltonian}, and we also use different units. The main simplification lies in considering only interactions between the nearest neighbours. In our case of a square lattice, $J_{ij}$ is just a constant. All $\bm{B}$, $\bm{D}_{ij}$ and $J_{ij}$ present in the \ref{eq:full-hamiltonian} have the unit of energy. To make the hamiltonian dimensioneless, we divide by $J$, abusing notation with: $\bm{D} \leftarrow \nicefrac{\bm{D}}{J}$ and $\bm{B} \leftarrow \nicefrac{\bm{B}}{J}$. We also assume a magnetic field oriented in the direction of the $z$-axis, resulting in the dimensionless Hamiltonian:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:MC-hamiltonian}
	H = - \sum_{<ij>} \bm{S}_i \cdot \bm{S}_j
	- \sum_{<ij>} \bm{D}_{ij} \cdot \left[\bm{S}_i \times \bm{S}_j\right] 
	- B \sum_i S_i^z,
\end{equation}

The magnetic field $B$ is expressed in simulation units as

\begin{equation}
	B \leftarrow \frac{\mu_B B[\text{T}]}{J},
\end{equation}

where $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton and $B[\text{T}]$ is the magnetic induction in Tesla. Similarly, temperature is made dimensionless by

\begin{equation}
	T \leftarrow \frac{k_B T[\text{K}]}{J},
\end{equation}

where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T[\text{K}]$ is the temperature in Kelvin. 

The temperature was reduced during the simulation up to the final temperature of $T=0.006$ in the units of $J$. The full dataset contains $9840$ magnetic configurations $3$ $3$-dimensional spin vectors on $200\times200$ square grid, simulated using periodic boundary conditions. Consider a unit vector $\bm{u}_{ij}$ pointing from $i$-th to $j$-th spin position. The $\bm{D}_{ij}$ vector is given by the $C_v$ symmetry, and has the following components:

\begin{align}
	\label{eq:DM-components}
	\bm{D} &= (0, D, 0)  && \text{for } \bm{u}_{ij} = (1, 0, 0), \\
	\bm{D} &= (0, -D, 0)  && \text{for } \bm{u}_{ij} = (-1, 0, 0), \\
	\bm{D} &= (-D, 0, 0) && \text{for } \bm{u}_{ij} = (0, 1, 0), \\
	\bm{D} &= (D, 0, 0) && \text{for } \bm{u}_{ij} = (0, -1, 0),
\end{align}

facilitating the formation of Neél-type skyrmions. Levels of external fields $D$ and $B$ were also held constant for each simulation. However, the values of $D$ and $B$ were systematically changed across different runs. Used values of $D$ ranged from $0$ to $2$, equidistantly spaced with a margin of $0.05$. Values of $B$ ranged from $0$ to $1$, similarly, spacing by $\approx 0.021$. For each pair of $D$ and $B$ values, we have $5$ independent configurations. The resulting magnetic configurations cover all $3$ phases and the transitions between them.

\subsection{Topological charge, magnetization}

We can describe magnetic configurations by different physical properties \cite{li2023magnetic}. In our context, the two most useful ones are the \emph{topological charge} and the \emph{magnetization}. While total magnetization is a well-known and straightforward characteristic of a magnetic configuration, the topological charge is a bit more nuanced but captures deeper structural properties.

Having a 2-D lattice, each site carries a spin vector $\bm{S}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ of unit length. The total magnetization is then defined as the average of all spins across the lattice:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{M} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \bm{S}_i,
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of spins, in the case of the ferromagnetic phase, all spins align in the same direction, and the magnetization vector has maximal norm, $|\bm{M}| \approx 1$. On the contrary, the global alignment vanishes or significantly weakens for more complex configurations such as spiral or skyrmion phases, and $|\bm{M}|$ is closer to zero.

A topological charge, also referred to as \emph{topological number} or \emph{skyrmion number}, measures how many times the spin configuration wraps around the unit sphere. For 2-D systems, it is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int \bm{S} \cdot \left( \frac{\partial \bm{S}}{\partial x} \times \frac{\partial \bm{S}}{\partial y} \right) \, dx\,dy.
\end{equation}

In the discrete case, this integral is approximated by the approach described in \cite{berg1981definition, yin2016topological}. A single skyrmion carries $Q = \pm 1$, where the sign depends on chirality. Ferromagnetic or spiral phase will have a topological charge close to zero.
