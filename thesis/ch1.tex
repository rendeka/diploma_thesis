\section{Physics of skyrmions}

Multiple distinct mechanisms can result in the emergence of magnetic skyrmions \cite{fert2017magnetic}. In some cases, skyrmions can emerge solely due to dipole interactions, but typically they are facilitated through an antisymmetric exchange. The consequences of such interaction were first observed as weak ferromagnetism in antiferromagnetic crystals in the early 20th century. A theoretical understanding of this phenomenon was provided by Igor Dzyaloshinskii, who showed that the interaction was due to the relativistic spin-lattice and magnetic dipole interaction. Later, Toru Moriya identified the spin-orbit coupling as the microscopic mechanism behind this interaction. In acknowledgment of their work, the antisymmetric exchange is referred to as \emph{Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya (DM) interaction}.

\subsection{Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction}

DM interaction plays an essential role in forming magnetic skyrmions and magnetic spirals in chiral magnets. It is a magnetic exchange interaction between particles with non-zero spin, characterized by the hamiltonian:

\begin{equation}
	\label{DM-interaction-hamiltonian}
	H^{\text{DM}}_{ij} = \bm{D}_{ij} \dot (\bm{S}_i \times \bm{S}_j),
\end{equation}

where $\bm{D}_{ij}$ is a Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya vector, and $\bm{S}_i$, $\bm{S}_j$ are spins. From the hamiltonian we see that the DM interaction prefers the perpendicular orientation of neighboring spins. The Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya vector $\bm{D}_{ij}$ is given by the symmetry of the system. 

The system symmetry dictates the direction of vector $\bm{D}_{ij}$, and influences the topology of skyrmions and spirals.

\subsection{Topological charge, magnetization}

We can describe magnetic configurations by different physical properties \cite{li2023magnetic}. The two most useful ones in our context are the \emph{topological charge} and the \emph{magnetization}. While total magnetization is a well known and straightforward characteristic of a magnetic configuration, the topological charge is bit more nuanced, but captures deeper structural properties.

Having a 2-D lattice where each site carries a spin vector $\mathbf{S}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ of unit length. The total magnetization is then defined as the average of all spins across the lattice:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{S}_i,
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of spins. In the case of the ferromagnetic phase, all spins align in the same direction, and the magnetization vector has maximal norm, $||\mathbf{M}||_2 \approx 1$. On the contrary, for more complex configurations such as spiral or skyrmion phases, the global alignment vanishes or significantly weakens, and $||\mathbf{M}||_2$ is closer to zero.

A topological charge, also refered to as \emph{topological number} or \emph{skyrmion number}, measures how many times the spin configuration wraps around the unit sphere. For 2-D systems it is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int \mathbf{S} \cdot \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{S}}{\partial x} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{S}}{\partial y} \right) \, dx\,dy.
\end{equation}

In the discrete case, this integral is approximated by approach described in \cite{berg1981definition, yin2016topological}. A single skyrmion carries $Q = \pm 1$, where the sign depends on chirality. Ferromagnetic or spiral phase will have a topological charge close to zero.

\subsection{Challenges of this dataset}

Looking at the single phase configurations in \ref{fig:phases}, the classification tasks may seem rather trivial, considering what are current image classification models capable of. However, the challenges of this dataset are multiple. Firstly, we want to predict the portion of each class in configurations where two phases are present without having any labels for such cases. Therefore it is a very non-trivial problem both to succesfully train and also evaluate the performance of a model on such cases. 

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/difficult_cases.pdf}
  \caption{Magnetic configurations near phase transitions.}
  \label{fig:difficult_cases}
\end{figure}

The second difficulty of this dataset is related to how the skyrmions and magnetic spirals change their size and shape under different levels of external fields. To ilustrate this, as well as to help the reader understand different phases, we have compiled figure \ref{fig:phase_map}. For example we see that by increasing magnetic field, skyrmions get smaller. Also with the small DM interaction levels the magnetic spirals take various volatile shapes, whereas for higher levels spirals arrange themselves into parallel regions. These dependencies carry a significant complications to our problem, that isn't directly connected to the phase changes, which is our main concern. Therefore, we use work only subset of the whole simulated dataset,t that is bounded by the red rectanles in the figure \ref{fig:magnetization_topology}. In this region, skyrmions and spirals have similar sizes and spirals are arranged in the parallel segments. This way we can focus on the effects of regularization on the model's performance on examples near the phase transitions.

\subsection{Data preprocessing}

Our first non-trivial task is to clean the dataset, separating the single-phases comfigurations from the mixed ones. We have done it heuristically utilizing the values of total magnetization and topological number. 

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{img/transitions_Q.pdf}
  \caption{Configurations near the phase transitions.}
  \label{fig:transitions_Q}
\end{figure}

In the figure \ref{fig:transitions_Q} we see the datapoint near the phase transitions, selected heuristically. These samples contain mostly the configuration with mixed magnetic phases, and we have no reliable way how to label them. Therefore, we can't use them easily during training, however our goal is to achieve reasonable predictions particularly on such data. 

Before training any neural network, we need to process the data into a form that both reflects the underlying physics and makes the training procedure more effective. In our case, the raw data are $200 \times 200 \times 3$ vector fields representing spin configurations, and the proper preprocessing is straight forward. From each configuration, we take only the $z$-component, which significantly reduces the data size, while maintaining all relevant information. This choice is also favours practical considerations, because this way we treat the configurations as greyscale images, comparable to experimental images obtained via electron microscopy.

Next, we normalize the pixel values of each image using min-max scaling to transform them into the range $[0, 1]$. This is known to improve convergence by reducing the risk of unnecessarily large or saturated gradients, especially during the early stages of training.

We also split the dataset into training, development, and testing sets. To do this, we utilize physical indicators: the average magnetization and the topological number. These quantities allow us to identify regions of the phase diagram where the configuration is clearly ferromagnetic, skyrmionic, or spiral. From these well-separated regions, we assign one-hot encoded labels: $(1, 0, 0)$ for ferromagnetic, $(0, 1, 0)$ for skyrmion, and $(0, 0, 1)$ for spiral phase. These cleanly labeled configurations are then randomly split into training, development, and test sets.



\subsection{Taking subset of this dataset}

To simplify our problem at first, we will restrict ourselves to a subset of the data, looking only at the strip of the original phase diagram. This way, we don't deal with the complications caused by the changing size of skyrmions and spirals. We focus on improving the performance of neural networks on the phase boudaries first, and can try so solve the problem of changing size later.
