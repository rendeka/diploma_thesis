\chapter{Mathematical backgroud}
\label{sec:ML-background}
Many real‑world problems are difficult to tackle with classical analytic methods, especially when the input is high‑dimensional and the target concepts are only loosely defined. Consider image recognition: each image is a matrix of real numbers and the goal may be to decide whether there is a dog, a cat, or an elephant. Hand‑crafted geometric features like pointy ears or a trunk can help, but such heuristics do not scale. Modern \emph{machine learning} (ML) and, in particular, \emph{deep learning} (DL) address this limitation by treating the task probabilistically and learning the relevant representations directly from data. In that, both ML and DL is identical with statistics, and the difference between these fields is rather philosofical, and we will refer to ML and DL models often as statistical models in this text. The latter fields are highly driven by improvement in predictive performance. This nature allows the the rapid evolution, with the application to various problems. To name a few, we can mention classification, regression, transcription, translation, anomaly detection, density estimation, and many more. The development in machine learning addresses ever more complex tasks, like image generation, protein folding, or large language models (LLMs) for context-appropriate, human-like text generation. These fields of application are, however, very remote in scale compared to our situation. For example, LLMs are often trained on any available digitized text, routinely using substantial parts of the whole internet content. Compared to that, our data options are much more limited, and our task is specialized and clearly defined. This allows us to use relatively simple statistical models and try to improve their performance with our understanding of their internal dynamics and interpretation of the inference.

\section{Probability and information theory}

Machine learning is grounded in the mathematical frameworks of probability theory and information theory, which provide the tools for modeling uncertainty and quantifying information. Machine learning deals with problems containing inherit uncertainties on multiple levels. The first level of uncertainty is in the input data. Sticking to the example, no two cat images are the same, therefore it is sensible to think about the image of a cat as a sample from a probability distribution of all cat images. The second level of uncertainty lies in the mapping from pixels of an image to the semantic label. We intuitively understand why developing a strict formulation for such mapping is hard. Instead, we can rely on the likelihood of specific outcomes based on distinct features in our input.

\paragraph{Conditional probability}
Suppose we have two random variables $X$ and $Y$. Conditional probability represents the probability of one event given that another event has already occurred. The conditional probability of $X$ given $Y$ is defined as \cite{klenke2013probability}:

\begin{equation} 
    P(X=x \mid Y=y) = \frac{P(X=x, Y=y)}{P(Y=y)}, 
\end{equation}

where we assume that $P(Y=y) > 0$, and $P(X=x, Y=y)$ denotes a joint probability that the random variables $X$ and $Y$ simultaneously take on values $x$ and $y$ respectively.

\paragraph{Likelihood}

The \emph{likelihood} \cite{klenke2013probability} tells us how probable a given set of observed data is, under a particular set of model parameters.

Let us consider a statistical model, represented as a probability distribution $p(x \mid \theta)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x$ is the observed data,
    \item $\theta$ are the parameters of the model.
\end{itemize}

While $p(x \mid \theta)$ is a function of $x$ for fixed $\theta$, likelihood treats the observed data $x$ as fixed and views it as a function of $\theta$. That is, we define the \emph{likelihood function} as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x) = p(x \mid \theta)
\end{equation}

If we observe multiple independent samples $x_1, \dots, x_m$, the total likelihood becomes a product of individual likelihoods:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) \equiv \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x_1, \dots, x_m) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p(x_i \mid \theta).
\end{equation}

This product can be numerically unstable, hence we usually work with the \emph{log-likelihood}, which transforms the product into a sum:
\begin{equation}
    \log \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p(x_i \mid \theta),
\end{equation}

This transformation preserves the extremal points and makes likelihood easier to deal with, especially when taking derivatives. 

\paragraph{Self information}
The main task of information theory is to quantify the amount of information in the signal. We can use its concepts in ML in order to describe a probability distribution and to compare information contained in two distributions \cite{bengio2017deep}. A very useful quantity describing the amount of information is called \emph{self-information}, denoted as $I$. The intuition behind self-information can be summarized in $3$ properties.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Likely events should carry low information content. For example, if an event $x$ occurs with high probability, it is not surprising, and thus $I(x)$ should be small. In the extreme case of a certain event (i.e., $p(x) = 1$), we expect $I(x) = 0$.
    \item Unlikely events should carry high information content, reflecting that rare events are more surprising and hence more informative.
    \item Independent events should have additive information content. Meaning, for two independent events $x$ and $y$ (i.e., $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y)$), the information of their joint occurrence should satisfy $I(x, y) = I(x) + I(y)$.
\end{itemize}

To satisfy all the above properties, self-information for a discrete variable is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:self-information}
	I(x) = - \ln P(x).
\end{equation}

While the self-information deals only with the single example, we can use it to quantify the uncertainty across the whole distribution. This notion is called \emph{Shannon entropy} \cite{bengio2017deep}, denoted as $H(P)$ and it is simply an expected value of self information across the probability distribution.

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:shannon-entropy} 
    H(P) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[I(x)] = - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\ln(P)]. 
\end{equation}

Shannon entropy tells us the expected amount of information while observing an event drawn from a given distribution. When dealing with two probability distributions, say $P$ (true target distribution) and $Q$ (model output distribution), we eventually need to measure how different they are.

A quantity that extends the idea of Shannon entropy to two distributions is the \emph{cross-entropy} \cite{bengio2017deep}. Cross-entropy measures how well the predicted distribution $Q$ matches the true distribution $P$. It can be seen as the expected amount of surprise when the true outcomes are sampled according to $P$ but are predicted using $Q$. It is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:cross-entropy} 
    H(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}{_x \sim P}[-\ln Q(x)] = - \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \ln Q(x). 
\end{equation}

If $P = Q$, the cross-entropy reduces to the Shannon entropy of $P$. In the context of machine learning, especially classification tasks, cross-entropy is often used as a loss function. It penalizes the model $Q$ for assigning low probability to the true label sampled from $P$.

A similar quantity is the \emph{Kullback-Leibler divergence} (KL divergence) \cite{bengio2017deep}, denoted as $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q)$. It quantifies the extra amount of information required to code samples from $P$ when using a coding scheme designed for $Q$. It can be interpreted as a measure of how much $Q$ diverges from $P$, and is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:kl-divergence} 
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q) = 
    \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \ln \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \ln \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}. 
\end{equation}

The KL divergence is always non-negative and equals zero if and only if $P = Q$ almost everywhere. Unlike true metrics, KL divergence is not symmetric, meaning that in general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q | P)$. It is useful to observe the relationship between cross-entropy, Shannon entropy, and KL divergence:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:cross-entropy-kl} 
    H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q).
\end{equation}

In the practical setting of supervised classification, we do not know the full data distribution $P$ and instead observe samples. Thus, we typically minimize the empirical cross-entropy, treating the labels as samples from $P$ and the model outputs as the distribution $Q$.

\subsection{Model training}
\label{subsec:training}

Machine learning builds on the concepts described in the previous sections of this appendix. In its essence, ML is trying to find a sufficiently accurate mapping between two distributions: the distribution of the input data and the distribution of their corresponding labels \textit{(sometimes referred to as targets)}. This mapping is a statistical model with its output distribution. In practice, we often expect the relationship between input and output distributions to be highly nonlinear, which needs to be reflected in the model's architecture. Training a statistical model is adjusting a subset of its parameters, such that the model's output distribution matches the true label distribution more closely. Typical ML model has, besides the trainable parameters, also \emph{hyperparameters}. These are values set before training that control the overall behavior of the learning process, such as learning rate, batch size, model architecture choices, or regularization strengths. Unlike trainable parameters, they are not learned from data but chosen through experimentation or systematic tuning.

\paragraph{Maximum likelihood estimation}
One of the most widely adopted and statistically grounded principles for training models is \emph{maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)} \cite{bengio2017deep}. The idea is that given a set of independent training examples $X = x_1, \dots, x_m$, we want to find the model parameters $\theta$ that maximize the likelihood of the observed data under the model distribution $p_{\text{model}}(x \mid \theta)$. Formally, the maximum likelihood estimator is defined as:

\begin{align}
\theta_{\text{MLE}} 
&= \arg\max_{\theta} p_{\text{model}}(X \mid \theta) \label{eq:mle}\\
&= \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_{\text{model}}(x_i \mid \theta)
\end{align}

Since optimizing a product of many probabilities can be numerically unstable, but we can use injectivity of the logarithm and maximize the log-likelihood instead:

\begin{align}
\theta_{\text{MLE}} 
&= \arg\max_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p_{\text{model}}(x_i \mid \theta)
\end{align}

By dividing by the number of training examples, we can express the objective as an expectation over the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_{\text{data}}$ defined by the training set:

\begin{align}
\theta_{\text{MLE}} 
&= \arg\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}} \left[ \log p_{\text{model}}(x \mid \theta) \right] \\
&= \arg\min_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}} \left[- \log p_{\text{model}}(x \mid \theta) \right] \label{eq:mle_nll} \\
&= \arg\min_{\theta} H\left(\hat{p}_{\text{data}}(x), p_{\text{model}}(x \mid \theta) \right) \label{eq:mle_cross_entropy} \\
&= \arg\min_{\theta} D_{\text{KL}}\left(\hat{p}_{\text{data}}(x) \,\|\, p_{\text{model}}(x \mid \theta) \right) \label{eq:mle_kl}
\end{align}

We see that if the empirical data distribution $\hat{p}_{\text{data}}$ accurately represents the true data distribution, the following objectives become mathematically equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maximizing the likelihood~\eqref{eq:mle_expectation},
    \item Minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL)~\eqref{eq:mle_nll},
    \item Minimizing the cross-entropy~\eqref{eq:mle_cross_entropy},
    \item Minimizing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence \eqref{eq:mle_kl}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Loss function}
In the previous subsection, we demonstrated that the optimal parameters of a statistical model can be found by minimizing various equivalent quantities, such as the negative log-likelihood, cross-entropy, or KL divergence. In machine learning, we formalize this by defining a function, known as the \emph{loss function} \cite{bengio2017deep}. Maximum likelihood estimation provides a principled foundation for designing the loss function. However, in practice, it is often advantageous to incorporate additional heuristics or prior knowledge into the loss, as this can lead to improved model performance and better generalization.

The \emph{focal loss} \cite{lin2017focal} is a good example of a heuritically modified loss function. This loss is useful when we have a class imbalance in the data. Focal loss modifies the standard cross-entropy loss by down-weighting easy examples and focusing the training on hard, misclassified ones. It is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:focal_loss}
	\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} = -\alpha_t (1 - p_k)^\gamma \log(p_k),
\end{equation}

where $p_k$ is the model's estimated probability for the true class, $\alpha_k$ is a weighting factor for class $k$, and $\gamma \geq 0$ is a tunable focusing parameter.

Once the loss function is defined, the task becomes an optimization problem: finding the global minimum of the loss in the high-dimensional space of the model’s parameters. Training the model is typically an iterative process that updates these parameters step-by-step to minimize the loss. We typically require the loss function to be differentiable, in order to apply gradient-based methods during minimization.

\paragraph{Gradient descent}
Most optimization methods used in training neural networks, are based on \emph{gradient descent} \cite{bengio2017deep}. The idea is to compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the model parameters and take a step in the direction that most decreases the loss. If $\theta$ denotes the current parameters and $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ the loss, then a single update step is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta),
\end{equation}

where $\alpha > 0$ is a hyperparameter called \emph{learning rate}, that controls the step size. 

The choice of the learning rate is one of the most critical hyperparameters for the successful training of neural networks. A learning rate that is too high can cause the optimization to diverge, while a learning rate that is too small can result in very slow convergence or getting stuck in suboptimal regions. In practice, it is beneficial not only to choose a good initial learning rate but also to adjust it during training. This approach is known as \emph{learning rate scheduling} or \emph{learning rate decay} and we discuss it further in \ref{subsec:decay-scheduling}.

Computing the gradient over the entire dataset can be very slow, if not impossible due to high memory requirements. Instead, we use an approximation based on randomly selected subsets of data. This leads to two widely used variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): The gradient is computed using a single randomly selected data point. While this is very efficient per update, the variance of the updates is high, which can make training unstable.
    \item Mini-batch gradient descent: A compromise between full-batch and stochastic methods. The gradient is computed over a small batch of data points, typically ranging from 16 to 512 samples. This approach is the standard in most modern applications, offering both stability and computational efficiency.
\end{itemize}
It it well established that the mini-batch gradient descent is the way to go if applicable, and the most popular optimization algorithms used in ML are its different extensions.

\paragraph{Backpropagation}
To efficiently compute gradients of the loss with respect to many of the model's parameters, the \emph{backpropagation} algorithm is used. Backpropagation \cite{bengio2017deep} applies the chain rule to propagate the loss gradient from the output layer backward through the network. Each layer computes the gradient of the loss with respect to its parameters and passes the gradient with respect to its inputs to the preceding layer. This recursive process allows the entire gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ to be computed in time linear in the number of layers.

\paragraph{Underfitting/overfitting}
\label{sec:uofitting}
A central challenge in training neural networks is ensuring that the model not only performs well on the training data but also generalizes to unseen examples. Two common, complementary problems are \cite{bengio2017deep}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underfitting:} The model is too simple or not sufficiently trained and fails to capture the underlying patterns in the data. Both training and test performance are poor.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting:} The model is too complex or trained for too long and starts to memorize the training data, including noise. While training performance is excellent, test performance degrades.
\end{itemize}
These phenomena can be visualized by tracking the training and validation loss over time. In underfitting, both losses remain high. In overfitting, the training loss continues to drop while the validation loss increases. If the model performs well on previously unseen data, we say it \emph{generalizes} well. In our case, we don't want achieve just low test error, we want to model to generalize even further a to behave well on unlabeled data from phase transitions containing a mixture of phases.
