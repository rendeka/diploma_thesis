\subsection{Challenges of this dataset}

Looking at the single phase configurations in \ref{fig:phases}, the classification tasks may seem rather trivial, especially considering what are current image classification models capable of. However, the challenges of this dataset are multiple. Firstly, we want to predict the portion of each class in configurations where two phases are present without having any labels for such cases. Therefore it is a very non-trivial problem both to succesfully train and also evaluate the performance of a model on such cases. 

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/difficult_cases.pdf}
  \caption{Magnetic configurations near phase transitions.}
  \label{fig:difficult_cases}
\end{figure}

The second difficulty of this dataset is related to how the skyrmions and magnetic spirals change their size and shape under different levels of external fields. To ilustrate this, as well as to help the reader understand different phases, we have compiled figure \ref{fig:phase_map}. For example we see that by increasing magnetic field, skyrmions get smaller. Also with the small DM interaction levels the magnetic spirals take various volatile shapes, whereas for higher levels spirals arrange themselves into parallel regions. These dependencies carry a significant complications to our problem, that isn't directly connected to the phase changes, which is our main concern. Therefore, we use work only subset of the whole simulated dataset,t that is bounded by the red rectanles in the figure \ref{fig:magnetization_topology}. In this region, skyrmions and spirals have similar sizes and spirals are arranged in the parallel segments. This way we can focus on the effects of regularization on the model's performance on examples near the phase transitions.

\subsection{Data preprocessing}

Our first non-trivial task is to clean the dataset, separating the single-phases comfigurations from the mixed ones. We have done it heuristically utilizing the values of total magnetization and topological number. 

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{img/transitions_Q.pdf}
  \caption{Configurations near the phase transitions.}
  \label{fig:transitions_Q}
\end{figure}

In the figure \ref{fig:transitions_Q} we see the datapoint near the phase transitions, selected heuristically. These samples contain mostly the configuration with mixed magnetic phases, and we have no reliable way how to label them. Therefore, we can't use them easily during training, however our goal is to achieve reasonable predictions particularly on such data. 

Before training any neural network, we need to process the data into a form that both reflects the underlying physics and makes the training procedure more effective. In our case, the raw data are $200 \times 200 \times 3$ vector fields representing spin configurations, and the proper preprocessing is straight forward. From each configuration, we take only the $z$-component, which significantly reduces the data size, while maintaining all relevant information. This choice is also favours practical considerations, because this way we treat the configurations as greyscale images, comparable to experimental images obtained via electron microscopy.

In our case, each pixel value on any image is in the range $[0, 1]$, which is ideal for the model training. When dealing with typical images, the pixel values are integers from $[0 \cdots 255]$, in which case using for example min-max scaling for all images is a very important step. It is known to improve convergence by reducing the risk of unnecessarily large or saturated gradients, especially during the early stages of training.

We also split the dataset into training, development, and testing sets. To do this, we utilize physical indicators: the average magnetization and the topological number. These quantities allow us to identify regions of the phase diagram where the configuration is clearly ferromagnetic, skyrmionic, or spiral. From these well-separated regions, we assign one-hot encoded labels: $(1, 0, 0)$ for ferromagnetic, $(0, 1, 0)$ for skyrmion, and $(0, 0, 1)$ for spiral phase. These cleanly labeled configurations are then randomly split into training, development, and test sets.

\subsubsection{Train/test split}
To evaluate how well a trained model generalizes, we need to test it on the previously unseen data. The most basic method for doing this is the \emph{train/test split}. The available dataset is split into two disjoint subsets: the \emph{training set}, used to update the model parameters, and the \emph{test set} to evaluate final performance.

It is useful to have also a third data set, so called \emph{validation set} to use for tuning hyperparameters or to perform early stopping during training.

\section{Perfomance evaluation}
We evaluate the performance of our models in multiple ways. The first metric we monitor is accuracy on the training and validation datasets. The accuracy is defined as the proportion of correctly predicted dominant phases. On the other hand, on the test set we accumulate the absolute value of error across the whole set. Once we reach a reasonable accuracy on these clean samples, our focus shifts to how well the models handle the cases near phase transitions. Since there are no true labels for these data, we introduce additional custom evaluation metrics, in order to quantitatively compare models on these data. We describe these in the section \ref{metric-definition}.

Finally, we reconstruct the phase diagram using trained models, and visualize their predictions near phase transitions to qualitatively inspect and compare their behavior. 

\subsection{Metrics definition}
\label{metric-definition}

Even though we have no labels for the data containing mixtures of phases, we can use 
In this subsection, we define metrics to evaluate model performance on data near the phase transitions between ferromagnetic $\rightarrow$ skyrmion and skyrmion $\rightarrow$ spiral phases. These configurations typically contain a mixture of phases within a single image, and we do not have a reliable way to assign ground truth labels to them. The goal is to define metrics that help us evaluate models even without labeled data. One useful idea is to penalize out-of-order predictions, based on known physical behavior across parameter sweeps. Since for each $(B, D)$ pair we always have five generated images, we can also include consistency-based terms in the metric. Additionally, we want to encourage smooth transitions in predicted probabilities as we move through the phase space.

The key observation we use is the following: for a fixed value of magnetic field $B$, increasing $D$ should lead to a continuous and monotonic transition from the ferromagnetic phase to the skyrmion phase, and with further increase of $D$, to the spiral phase. This gives us a way to evaluate predictions against the physically expected ordering of phase transitions.

To capture these ideas, we define three metrics: out-of-order error, in-group variance, and transition smoothness.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Out-of-order error} measures how well the predicted ordering of phase transitions matches the expected progression. For a fixed $B$, we sort the images by increasing $D$ to define a reference order $r$. We then use the model to predict the dominant phase probabilities and sort the images according to these predictions to get the predicted order $\hat{r}$. We compare $\hat{r}$ and $r$ using the Euclidean norm:
\begin{equation}
O = \left| \hat{r} - r \right|.
\end{equation}
A lower value of $O$ indicates better alignment with the expected phase transition ordering.

\item \textbf{In-group variance} quantifies the consistency of predictions within groups of five equivalent images simulated under the same $(B, D)$ parameters. For each rank $r$, we compute the variance of the predicted probability vectors $p_i$ within the group and sum over all ranks:
\begin{equation}
V = \sum_r \mathrm{Var}(p_i \mid \hat{r}_i = r).
\end{equation}
A lower value of $V$ means the model consistently predicts similar probabilities for physically identical inputs.

\item \textbf{Transition smoothness} measures how smoothly the predicted probabilities evolve as we move across the ranks. For each rank $r$, we compute the mean predicted probability vector $\mu_r$, and then calculate the average absolute change between successive means:
\begin{equation}
S = \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{r=1}^{N-1} \left\| \mu_{r+1} - \mu_r \right\|.
\end{equation}
A lower value of $S$ indicates smoother transitions and suggests that the model is sensitive to subtle, continuous changes in the spin configurations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature Map Visualizations}
We can gain insight into a trained convolutional model by visualizing its feature maps. We start with a given input image and pass it through the model. At each convolutional layer, the learned filters are applied to the input, producing a set of feature maps on the output. By visualizing these feature maps, we can get an idea of what kind of features the model is focusing on at different depths. This can help us make more informed decisions when designing or selecting model architectures.

We have also implemented the Grad-CAM method \cite{Selvaraju_2019}, which produces a coarse localization heatmap highlighting the regions in the input that contribute the most to the prediction. The idea is to compute the gradient of the output with respect to the feature maps in the last convolutional layer and use this information to weigh the feature maps. The result is a heatmap that can be overlaid on the input image to indicate important regions.

However, in our case, Grad-CAM did not provide any particularly useful information. This is because the method requires labeled data and works best when specific regions in the image are responsible for the prediction. In our dataset, especially in the clean, single-phase configurations, the relevant features are distributed uniformly across the image, and no single region carries dominant information. As a result, the generated heatmaps were mostly uniform and did not help us interpret the model's behavior in a meaningful way.

\section{Results on the subset of data}
\subsection{Fully connected network}
In the paper \cite{iakovlev2018supervised}, the largest lattice authors used was $128 \times 128$. Their best-performing model consisted of a single hidden layer with $64$ neurons and no activation function applied to that layer. They normalized the input by the total magnetization, stating that this step was especially important at the beginning of training, as without it the model would often output only $0$ or $1$. However, this kind of normalization is non-standard and potentially risky for configurations with magnetization close to zero, which can occur in both skyrmion and spiral phases.

The output layer consisted of $3$ neurons with sigmoid activation. This output is not automatically normalized to sum to $1$. Since we want the outputs to represent class probabilities summing to $1$, we manually normalize them, and we will also experiment with a more standard classification activation softmax, which enforces the normalization automatically.

They used mean squared error as the loss function and employed early stopping once a specific accuracy threshold was reached, while also monitoring validation loss to avoid overfitting. The optimizer used was stochastic gradient descent with momentum.
