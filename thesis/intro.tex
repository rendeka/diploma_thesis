\chapwithtoc{Introduction}

\section*{Skyrmion}

The term \emph{skyrmion} originates from quantum field theory, describing topologically stable field configurations with particle-like properties. Tony Skyrme introduced it as a potential model of the nucleon in 1961. Although this model was incorrect, skyrmions, as topological objects, re-emerged in various other fields. This thesis deals with \emph{magnetic skyrmions}, which are 2-D vertex-like structures characterized by rotationally symmetric, continuously changing spin vectors from their middle toward their edges. The existence of magnetic skyrmions was theoretically predicted in 1989 \cite{bogdanov1989thermodynamically}, and for the first time experimentally observed in 2009 \cite{muhlbauer2009skyrmion} in chiral magnets, which are magnetic materials with broken inversion symmetry. Besides chiral magnets, magnetic skyrmions were later observed in ultra-thin films and multilayer structures. The non-trivial topology of skyrmions is tightly related to their stability. Skyrmions can withstand perturbations caused by thermal fluctuations or material defects without corrupting their structure. With their small size and very energy-efficient manipulation, this stability makes them an interesting object for application. A critical experimental feat was achieved in \cite{moreau2016additive} by stabilizing skyrmions at room temperature with the diameter in sub-$\SI{100}{\nano\meter}$ ranges. Skyrmions gained particular interest in the field of neuromorphic computing \cite{chen2020nanoscale} and in the development of magnetic memory devices.

\section*{Problem statement}

In this thesis, we consider a setup analogous to one in chiral magnets, where the formation of magnetic skyrmions is conditional on two external fields. The first is the \emph{Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya (DM)} interaction, which supports the anti-parallel orientation of neighbouring spins, and the second is the \emph{magnetic field (B)}, which helps with the stabilization of individual skyrmions. By changing these external fields, skyrmions undergo a continuous transition towards either \emph{ferromagnet} or \emph{magnetic spiral} phase. A schematic illustration of a phase diagram is in \ref{fig:phase_diagram_schematic}.

However, there are no strict boundaries between these phases as they transition continuously into each other. The nature of these phase transitions makes them challenging to study analytically. A promising approach to better understand these transitions involves \emph{machine learning (ML)}. Machine learning models proved their ability to recognize complex patterns in high-dimensional data in the recent era. We want to use this ability to precisely classify magnetic configurations, which can help us better understand the phase diagram.

This thesis aims to explore different ML models and study the effect of various \emph{regularization} techniques. We strive to improve the performance of these models so that they will also be able to recognize mixtures of these phases that occur near the phase transitions. 

This approach, however, is predicated on one additional step: data generation. To successfully train a model, we first need a sufficient amount of relevant data. For our case, we need magnetic configurations containing all $3$ phases and the transitions between them. Our group has already created such a dataset, so data generation wasn't part of this thesis.

\section*{Current approaches and our contribution}

Multiple ML approaches have already been applied to this problem in \cite{iakovlev2018supervised}, where the authors showed that a simple dense neural network can classify these $3$ phases with a reasonable accuracy. However, with the rise of \emph{deeplearning (DL)}, we have many more tools at our disposal, compared to those considered in the mentioned paper. We will try to improve on these results by experimenting with various architectures. We focus on applying different regularization techniques with the potential to improve performance, especially in the problematic cases near the phase transitions.

We will delve deeper into different ML model architectures in later chapters. Here, I want to skip a bit ahead and discuss the overall scope of our ML approach. We are dealing with images of magnetic configurations, essentially trying to evaluate how much each phase is in the given image. There are multiple ways to approach such a problem. The most straightforward one, and the one we choose, is to use \emph{soft classification}. Soft classification is a type of classification where we try to learn the probability distribution over classes, instead of hard labels. Another approach for our problem could be segmentation and object detection. Segmentation means that we try to classify each pixel separately, while in object detection, we try to localize individual skyrmions or spirals with bounding boxes precisely. These two approaches have potential for more accurate predictions, but also require a significant investment in data preparation. Moreover, the main goal of this thesis is to study regularizations for which the soft classification offers a perfect setup. In addition, it is possible that our findings using soft classification, will also be transferable to the image segmentation and object detection, since we study ways to improve models' performance for a concrete type of data.
