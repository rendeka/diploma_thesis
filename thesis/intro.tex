\chapwithtoc{Introduction}

\section*{What are skyrmions?}

Skyrmion originates from particle theory, describing topologically stable field configurations with particle-like properties. Tony Skyrme introduced it as a potential model of the nucleon in 1961. Although this model turned out incorrect, skyrmions, as topological objects re-emerged in various other fields. This thesis deals with \emph{magnetic skyrmions}, which are 2-D vertex-like structures characterized by rotationally symmetric, continuously changing spin vectors from their middle toward their edges. Existence of magnetic skyrmions was theoretically predicted in 1989 \cite{bogdanov1989thermodynamically}, and for the first time experimentally observed in 2009 \cite{mühlbauer2009skyrmion} in chiral magnets, which are magnetic materials with broken inversion symmetry. Besides chiral magnets, magnetic skyrmions were later observed also in ultra-thin films and multilayer structures. The non-trivial topology of skyrmions gives them stability, making them able to withstand perturbations caused by thermal fluctuations or material defects. Stability, with their small size (10-100 nanometers scale) and very energy-efficient manipulation, makes them an interesting object for application in spintronics. Important experimental feat was achieved in \cite{moreau2016additive} by stabilizing Skyrmions at room temperature with the diameter in sub $\SI{100}{\nano\meter}$ ranges. Skyrmions gained particular interest in the field of neuromorphic computing \cite{chen2020nanoscale} and in the development of magnetic memory devices.

\section*{Problem statement}

In this thesis, we consider a setup analogous to one in chiral magnets, where the formation of magnetic skyrmions is conditional on two external fields. The first and most important is the so-called \emph{Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya (DM)} interaction, which supports the anti-parallel orientation of neighbouring spins. The second is the \emph{magnetic field (B)}, which is necessary to isolate and stabilize individual skyrmions.

Assuming a constant temperature, low enough to allow skyrmions to form, their size depends on the levels of the mentioned external fields. Also, with changing external fields, skyrmions undergo a continuous phase transition towards a \emph{ferromagnet} or a degenerate skyrmion phase called a \emph{magnetic spiral}. This nature makes it difficult to define strict phase boundaries, which is why a statistical approach can be beneficial. Our idea is based on two steps: The first is a Monte Carlo simulation of spin configuration at different levels of oiakovlev2018supervisedf external fields. We generate a number of configurations for all 3 phases, also capturing the continuous phase transitions. We take a projection to the $z$-axis for each spin in the configuration and treat this projection as an image. The second step is deploying a statistical model, using images as inputs. Later step counts on the proven ability of machine-learning (ML) models to recognize complex patterns in high-dimensional data. With this approach, we aim to achieve more precise classification, resulting in a deeper understanding of the phase diagram and providing useful insight for the general study of magnetic systems with topological structures.

\section*{Approach to solve the problem}

Multiple machine-learning approaches have already been applied to this problem in \citet{iakovlev2018supervised}, where it was proved that a simple dense neural network is able to classify these 3-phases with reasonable accuracy. However, the field of ML provides much richer options than those considered in the paper. We will build, and try to improve on these results by experimenting with various architectures and systematically exploring numerous options one has when defining and training a statistical model. We specialize in evaluating various regularization techniques such as data augmentation or dropout Monte Carlo.

ML models are often considered black boxes, which is often true, especially in the result-driven fields. Therefore, we prefer models with good interpretation and explore our options systematically. This way, we can gain a general insight into how the model behaves and why, and with such an insight, we are then able to make rational choices in model architecture and have more power in the interpretation of the results. Therefore, this thesis will also be a very useful resource for the application of ML models in this problem for real experimental data obtained, e.g., by Lorenz transmission electron microscopy or spin-polarized scanning tunneling microscopy.
