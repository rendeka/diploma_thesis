\chapwithtoc{Introduction}

\section*{What are skyrmions?}

Skyrmion originates from particle theory, describing topologically stable field configurations with particle-like properties. Tony Skyrme introduced it as a potential model of the nucleon in 1961. Although this model turned out incorrect, skyrmions, as topological objects re-emerged in various other fields. This thesis deals with \emph{magnetic skyrmions}, which are 2-D vertex-like structures characterized by rotationally symmetric, continuously changing spin vectors from their middle toward their edges. Existence of magnetic skyrmions was theoretically predicted in 1989 \cite{bogdanov1989thermodynamically}, and for the first time experimentally observed in 2009 \cite{muhlbauer2009skyrmion} in chiral magnets, which are magnetic materials with broken inversion symmetry. Besides chiral magnets, magnetic skyrmions were later observed also in ultra-thin films and multilayer structures. The non-trivial topology of skyrmions is tightly related to their stability. Skyrmions can withstand perturbations caused by thermal fluctuations or material defects, without corrupting their structure. This stability, together with their small size and very energy-efficient manipulation, makes them an interesting object for application. Important experimental feat was achieved in \cite{moreau2016additive} by stabilizing Skyrmions at room temperature with the diameter in sub $\SI{100}{\nano\meter}$ ranges. Skyrmions gained particular interest in the field of neuromorphic computing \cite{chen2020nanoscale} and in the development of magnetic memory devices.

\section*{Problem statement}

In this thesis, we consider a setup analogous to one in chiral magnets, where the formation of magnetic skyrmions is conditional on two external fields. The first and most important is the so-called \emph{Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya (DM)} interaction, which supports the anti-parallel orientation of neighbouring spins. The second is the \emph{magnetic field (B)}, which is necessary to isolate and stabilize individual skyrmions. By changing these external fields, skyrmions undergo a continuous phase transition towards a \emph{ferromagnet} or a degenerate skyrmion phase called a \emph{magnetic spiral}. A schematic ilustration of a phase diagram is in the figure \ref{fig:phase_diagram_schematic}.

However, in reality there are no strict boundaries between these phases, and they transition continuously into each other. The nature of these phase transitions makes them difficult to study analytically. An interesting approach to better understand these transitions involves \emph{machine learning (ML)}. Especially in recent years, ML models have proven their ability to recognize complex patterns in high-dimensional data. We can use this ability to achieve precise classification of magnetic configurations, which can then result in deeper understanding of the phase diagram.

In this thesis, we aim to explore various ML models and \emph{regularization} techniques, as an attempt to improve their perforamnce in classification the three magnetic phases, while also being able to recongize mixtures of these phases that occurs near the phase transitions. 

This approach involves two distinct steps. Firstly, we need the relevant data. At the time we started to working on this thesis, we already had a dataset of $9840$ magnetic configurations obtained by Monte-Carlo simulations at different levels of external fields. These data were simulated to capture all $3$ phases, as well as the transitions between them, laying a solid foundation for our next work. The second  step, and the main concern of this thesis, is to build and optimize various statistical models for classification of the simulated configurations.


\section*{Current approaches and our contribution}

Multiple ML approaches have already been applied to this problem in \cite{iakovlev2018supervised}, where the authors proved that a simple dense neural network is able to classify these $3$ phases with reasonable accuracy. However, with a rise of \emph{deeplearning (DL)}, we have much more tools at our disposal, compared to those considered in the mentioned paper. We will try to improve on these results by experimenting with various architectures and systematically exploring numerous regularization techniques one has when defining and training a statistical model. We focus on applying various regularization techniques which have potential for improving performace especially in the problematic cases near the phase transitions.

ML models are often considered black boxes, which is often true, especially in the result-driven fields. Therefore, we prefer models with good interpretation and explore our options systematically. This way, we can gain a general insight into how the model behaves and why, and with such an insight, we are then able to make rational choices in model architecture and have more power in the interpretation of the results. Therefore, this thesis can serve as a resource for the application of ML models in this problem for real experimental data obtained by Lorenz transmission electron microscopy or spin-polarized scanning tunneling microscopy.

We will dwelve deeper into different ML model architectures in later chapters. Here I want to skip a bit ahead and discuss the overall scope of our ML approach. We are dealing with images of magnetic configurations, trying to evaluate how much of each phase is in the given image. There are multiple ways how to approach such problem. The most straight forward one is to treat it as a \emph{soft classification}. Soft classification is a little less common type of classification, where we try to learn the probability distribution over classes, instead of hard labels. Another interesting option are be to apply segmentation or object detection, where in segmentation means we try to classify each pixel separatelly and in object detection we try to locate each individual skyrmion or spiral. These two approaches have potential for more accurate predictions with easier interpretation, however they also require huge investment in terms of data preparation. Moreover, the main goal of this thesis is to study regularizations for which the soft classification offers a perfect setup. In addition, it is likely that our findings about regularization will be trasferable also for the image segmentation and object detection tasks for our problem.
