
\chapwithtoc{Conclusion}

In this thesis, we studied the use of neural networks to classify $3$ magnetic phases in spin configurations obtained via Monte Carlo simulation. We started by establishing the settings enabling the machine learning model to reliably recognize the $3$ phases: ferromagnet, skyrmion, and magnetic spiral on clean, single-phase configurations.  Our main goal was to identify regularization and modeling strategies that improve the model's behavior in phase transitions, where the configurations contain a mixture of phases.

Our experiments showed that simple convolutional networks consistently outperformed dense networks and even more sophisticated CNN variants, including Squeeze-and-Excite or CBAM blocks. We also observed that deeper networks perform worse on phase transitions despite excellent test-set performance. Among optimizers, RMSprop combined with a plateau-based learning rate schedule delivered the best results, significantly improving convergence speed and training stability. Using global average pooling instead of flattening significantly reduced the number of parameters, while maintaining the same performance on the test set and improving the performance on the skyrmion-spiral transition.

Regarding data augmentation, CutMix achieved the most substantial improvement on the skyrmion–spiral phase transition, improving the out-of-order metric by about $60\%$ compared to the best non-augmented baseline. However, it also degraded performance on the ferromagnet–skyrmion transition, where even unaugmented models achieved perfect results, as measured by our metric. In contrast, MixUp did not significantly affect the skyrmion–spiral transition but slightly improved behavior on the ferromagnetic–skyrmion boundary. Overall, the best results were obtained by a model trained on the CutMixes of skyrmion and spiral lattices, together with MixUps of ferromagnets and skyrmion lattices.

We also investigated dropout as a regularization technique. Contrary to expectations, dropout neither improved test performance nor helped with phase transitions. It tended to negate the positive effects of augmentation. Using Monte Carlo dropout at inference did not noticeably change model behavior.

Overall, we have explored many machine learning methods and systematically mapped which approaches work well and which do not for this classification task. Most importantly, we have shown the impact of non-physical data augmentations on model performance during the phase transitions.
