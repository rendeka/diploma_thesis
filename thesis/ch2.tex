\section{Machine learning}
Many problems are challenging to define or solve using classical mathematical tools. Imagine going back to the early 2000s, when you were given a set of images represented by a grid of real numbers, and your task was to devise a rule to decide whether there was a dog, a cat, or an elephant in a given picture. At first glance, it doesn't seem the mathematical framework is well-suited to tackle such questions. You may try to develop some distinct geometrical features, for example, cats have pointy ears, and elephants have a trunk. Then, you can try to find these shapes in the images, and you may have some success with such an approach. If I ask you to do the same, not with $3$ categories, but with $1000$ different classes, the problem seems impossible to solve. Today, we know such things are possible, mainly due to the rapid advancement in the field of \emph{Machine learning (ML)}. At its core, machine learning looks at such a problem from a probabilistic view. In this chapter, we aim to introduce the main theoretical concepts as well as practical ideas that lay the basis for the practical part of this thesis. We use the terms \textit{machine} and \textit{statistical} learning interchangeably in the spirit of Larry Wasserman's quote: "There are no machines, only statistics."
Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the difference between these two fields. Even though these fields are identical in their intent (learning from the data) and their mathematical base, their history, culture, and future development direction set them apart quite substantially. While traditional statistics leans on interpretability, formal inference, and hypothesis testing, intending to most rigorously describe the data-generating process, the development in machine learning is highly driven by improvement in predictive performance. This nature allows the field of ML to evolve rapidly, with the application to various problems. To name a few, we can mention classification, regression, transcription, translation, anomaly detection, density estimation, etc. The development in machine learning addresses ever more complex tasks, like image generation, protein folding, or large language models (LLMs) for context-appropriate, human-like text generation. These fields of application are, however, very remote in scale compared to our situation. For example, LLMs are often trained on any available digitized text, routinely using substantial parts of the whole internet content. Compared to that, our data options are much more limited, and our task is specialized and clearly defined. This also encourages us to adopt some ways of thinking closer to more traditional statistical approaches. Our setup allows us to use relatively simple statistical models and try to improve their performance with our understanding of their internal dynamics and interpretation of the inference.

\section{Probability and information theory}

Machine learning mainly uses the language of two closely related disciplines, probability and information theory. The reason can be evident from the example we gave at the beginning of this chapter. Machine learning deals with problems containing inherited uncertainties on multiple levels. The first level of uncertainty is in the input data. Sticking to our example, no two cats are the same, and cats can be caught in pictures in various positions from different angles, distances, and backgrounds. Therefore, it is sensible to think about the image of a cat as an example from a probability distribution of all cat images. The second level of uncertainty lies in the mapping from pixels of an image to the semantic label, like a cat. We intuitively understand why developing a strict formulation for such mapping is hard. Instead, we can rely on the likelihood of specific outcomes based on distinct features in our input. In the rest of this section, we will introduce the key concepts from probability and information theory that we will use in further sections. Regarding topics in probability, we use \cite{klenke2013probability} as a reference, and \cite{bengio2017deep} for the information theory and ML parts. The reader may refer to this text for more thorough and rigorous explanations.

\subsection{Sample space}
An important concept in probability theory, setting the ground for further definitions, is \emph{sample space}, denoted as $\Omega$. Suppose we do an experiment with a probabilistic outcome, like a single coin toss. Sample space is a set of all possible outcomes of such an experiment, in this case $\Omega = \{\text{head}, \text{tail}\}$.

\subsection{Random variable}
A random variable is an important concept in ML because it provides a valid mathematical framework to represent noisy, real-world data. Random variable $X$ is a mapping from the sample space $\Omega$ into some target space (typically $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{Z}$). We can treat an example from the data as a realization of a random variable. Following the single coin toss example, we can define $X$ simply by $X(\text{head}) = 0$ and $X(\text{tail}) = 1$. The instance of a random variable is denoted by $x$ and equals $x = X(\omega)$ for some $\omega \in \Omega$, in our case $x \in \{0, 1\}$.

\subsection{Probability distribution}
Every random variable is closely tied to some probability distribution. Probability distribution quantifies the probability of each state a random variable can take. In the case of a fair coin, it would give both outcomes the same probability $\nicefrac{1}{2}$. Probability distribution can be either continuous or discrete. Both cases are relevant in most ML applications. Let us briefly introduce both of them.

\subsubsection{Discrete probability distribution}
The discrete probability distribution is characterized by a finite (or countably infinite) set of states. We usually describe it by a \emph{probability mass function} (PMF), denoted by $P$. It assigns a probability to each state of a random variable. We denote $P(X = x) \equiv P(x)$ a probability that a random variable $X$ takes a value $x$. There are three natural properties a proper PMF has to follow:

\begin{align}
1.\quad&\operatorname{dom}(P) = \mathcal{X}, \\
2.\quad&P(\bm{x}) \geq 0 \quad  \forall \bm{x} \in \mathcal{X}, \\
3.\quad&\sum_{\bm{x} \in \mathcal{X}} P(\bm{x}) = 1.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Continuous probability distribution}
Continuous probability distribution is described by \emph{probability density function (PDF)}, denoted by $p$. Analogously to the discrete case, PDF gives us a probability that $x \in [a, b]$ as $\int_a^b p(x) dx$.
\begin{align}
1.\quad&\operatorname{dom}(p) = \mathcal{X}, \\
2.\quad&p(\bm{x}) \geq 0 \quad  \forall \bm{x} \in \mathcal{X}, \\
3.\quad&\int_{\mathcal{X}} p(\bm{x}) d\bm{x} = 1.
\end{align}

All the previously introduced concepts extend naturally to multiple dimensions, with the notion of \emph{random vector}. A random vector is a vector-valued function, where each component is a random variable defined on the same underlying sample space. We often need to work with multiple random vectors, each representing different but potentially related parts of the data. We often need \emph{conditional probability} to model these relationships properly.

\subsection{Conditional probability}
Suppose we have two random variables $(\mathbf{X}$ and $(\mathbf{Y}$. Conditional probability measures the probability of an event given that another event has occurred. Formally, the conditional probability of $X$ given $Y$ is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    P(X=x \mid Y=y) = \frac{P(X=x, Y=y)}{P(Y=y)}, 
\end{equation}

where we assume that $P(Y=y) > 0$, and $P(X=x, Y=y)$ denotes a joint probability that the random variables $X$ and $Y$ simultaneously take on values $x$ and $y$ respectively.

\subsection{Expectation, variance, covariance} The expectation (or expected value) of a random variable $X$ captures the average outcome if the experiment were repeated infinitely many times. For a discrete variable:

\begin{equation} 
    \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} x P(X=x). 
\end{equation}

Variance measures how much the values of $X$ are spread around the mean:

\begin{equation} 
    \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2]. 
\end{equation}

Covariance extends this idea to two random variables and measures how much they change together:

\begin{equation} 
    \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])]. 
\end{equation}

When the covariance is positive, $X$ and $Y$ tend to increase together; when it is negative, one tends to decrease as the other increases.

\subsection{Statistical (in)dependence} 
Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are said to be statistically independent if knowing the value of one provides no information about the other. Mathematically, $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if:

\begin{equation} 
    P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x) P(Y=y),
\end{equation}

for all possible values of $x$ and $y$.
When this condition does not hold, $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. Understanding (in)dependence is crucial when designing machine learning models, especially when modeling joint distributions or assuming factorization in probabilistic models.

\subsection{Likelihood}

In statistics and machine learning, \emph{likelihood} is a function that tells us how probable a given set of observed data is, under a particular set of model parameters.

Let us consider a statistical model, represented as a probability distribution $p(x \mid \theta)$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x$ is the observed data,
    \item $\theta$ are the parameters of the model.
\end{itemize}

While $p(x \mid \theta)$ is a function of $x$ for fixed $\theta$, likelihood treats the observed data $x$ as fixed and views it as a function of $\theta$. That is, we define the \emph{likelihood function} as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x) = p(x \mid \theta)
\end{equation}

If we observe multiple independent samples $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}$, the total likelihood becomes a product of individual likelihoods:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) \equiv \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta).
\end{equation}

This product can be numerically unstable, hence we usually work with the \emph{log-likelihood}, which transforms the product into a sum:
\begin{equation}
    \log \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p(x^{(i)} \mid \theta),
\end{equation}

This transformation preserves the extremal points and makes likelihood easier to deal with, especially when taking derivatives. 

\subsection{Self information}
The main task of information theory is to quantify the amount of information in the signal. We can use its concepts in ML in order to describe a probability distribution and to compare information contained in two distributions. A very useful quantity describing the amount of information is called \emph{self-information}, denoted as $I$. The intuition behind self-information can be summarized in $3$ properties.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Likely events should carry low information content. For example, if an event $x$ occurs with high probability, it is not surprising, and thus $I(x)$ should be small. In the extreme case of a certain event (i.e., $p(x) = 1$), we expect $I(x) = 0$.
    \item Unlikely events should carry high information content, reflecting that rare events are more surprising and hence more informative.
    \item Independent events should have additive information content. Meaning, for two independent events $x$ and $y$ (i.e., $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y)$), the information of their joint occurrence should satisfy$ I(x, y) = I(x) + I(y)$.
\end{itemize}

To satisfy all the above properties, self-information for a discrete variable is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:self-information}
I(x) = - \ln P(x).
\end{equation}

For the continuous random variable, self-information is defined analogously; the practical difference is that self-information can also be negative. Note that the logarithm in the above definition can have a different base. Using the natural logarithm implies that self-information is measured in \emph{nats}, whereas using the \textit{(from an information theory perspective, more traditional)} base 2 means the unit of self-information is \emph{bits}.

While the self-information deals only with the single example, we can use it to quantify the uncertainty across the whole distribution. This notion is called \emph{Shannon entropy}, denoted as $H(P)$ and it is simply an expected value of self information across the probability distribution.

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:shannon-entropy} 
    H(P) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[I(x)] = - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[\ln(P)]. 
\end{equation}
In other words, Shannon entropy tells us the expected amount of information while observing an event drawn from a given distribution. When dealing with two probability distributions, say $P$ (true target distribution) and $Q$ (model output distribution), we eventually need to measure how "different" or "close" they are.

A quantity that extends the idea of Shannon entropy to two distributions is the \emph{cross-entropy}. Cross-entropy measures how well the predicted distribution $Q$ matches the true distribution $P$. It can be seen as the expected amount of surprise when the true outcomes are sampled according to $P$ but are predicted using $Q$. It is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:cross-entropy} 
    H(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}{_x \sim P}[-\ln Q(x)] = - \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \ln Q(x). 
\end{equation}

If $P = Q$, the cross-entropy reduces to the Shannon entropy of $P$. In the context of machine learning, especially classification tasks, cross-entropy is often used as a loss function. It penalizes the model $Q$ for assigning low probability to the true label sampled from $P$.

Another important quantity is the \emph{Kullback-Leibler divergence} (KL divergence), denoted as $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q)$. It quantifies the extra amount of information required to code samples from $P$ when using a coding scheme designed for $Q$. It can be interpreted as a measure of how much $Q$ diverges from $P$, and is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:kl-divergence} 
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q) = 
    \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \ln \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \ln \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}. 
\end{equation}

The KL divergence is always non-negative and equals zero if and only if $P = Q$ almost everywhere. Unlike true metrics, KL divergence is not symmetric, meaning that in general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q | P)$.

It is useful to observe the relationship between cross-entropy, Shannon entropy, and KL divergence:

\begin{equation} 
    \label{eq:cross-entropy-kl} 
    H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P | Q).
\end{equation}

Thus, minimizing the cross-entropy between $P$ and $Q$ is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence, up to an additive constant independent of $Q$.

In the practical setting of supervised classification, we do not know the full data distribution $P$ and instead observe samples. Thus, we typically minimize the empirical cross-entropy, treating the labels as samples from $P$ and the model outputs as the distribution $Q$.

\section{Machine learning}
Machine learning builds on the concepts mentioned in the previous section. In its essence, ML is trying to find a sufficiently accurate mapping between two distributions: the distribution of the input data and the distribution of their corresponding labels \textit{(sometimes referred to as targets)}. This mapping is a statistical model with its output distribution. In most practical cases, we expect the relationship between input and output distributions to be highly nonlinear, which needs to be reflected in the model's architecture. Training a statistical model is adjusting a subset of its parameters, such that the model's output distribution matches the true label distribution more closely. We will look closer at the model training in section \ref{subsec:training}.

\subsection{Model training}
\label{subsec:training}

\subsubsection{Maximum likelihood estimation}
One of the fundamental principles for training statistical models is called \emph{maximum likelihood estimation} (MLE). The core idea is simple: given a set of independent training examples $X = {x^{(1)}, \ldots, x^{(m)}}$, we seek model parameters $\theta$ that maximize the likelihood of the observed data under the model distribution $p_{\text{model}}(x; \theta)$. Formally, the maximum likelihood estimator is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
    \theta_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{\theta} p_{\text{model}}(X; \theta) = \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{i=1}^{m} p_{\text{model}}(x^{(i)}; \theta). 
\end{equation}

Since optimizing a product of many probabilities can be numerically unstable, it is standard to maximize the log-likelihood instead, transforming the objective into a sum:

\begin{equation} 
    \theta_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log p_{\text{model}}(x^{(i)}; \theta). 
\end{equation}

By dividing by the number of training examples, we can express the objective as an expectation over the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_{\text{data}}$ defined by the training set:

\begin{equation} 
    \theta_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}{x \sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}} [\log p_{\text{model}}(x; \theta)]. 
\end{equation}

An alternative interpretation of maximum likelihood is minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the empirical data distribution $\hat{p}{\text{data}}$ and the model distribution $p{\text{model}}$. Because the KL divergence measures how much one distribution differs from another, minimizing it aligns the model predictions as closely as possible to the data. This leads to the equivalent objective of minimizing the negative log-likelihood, commonly referred to as the \emph{loss function} in machine learning practice.

\subsubsection{Loss function}
To meaningfully adjust the model's parameters, we need a way to compare its output distribution with the distribution of the true labels. ML defines such a metric as \emph{loss} or \emph{loss function}.

Once we have a defined loss function that quantifies how wrong the model’s predictions are, we are facing the optimization problem of finding a global minimum of a loss function in a high dimensional space of model's parameters. Model training is typically an iterative process of updating the model parameters to minimize this loss.
\subsubsection{Gradient descent}
Most optimization methods used in training neural networks, are based on \emph{gradient descent}. The idea is to compute the gradient of the loss function with respect to the model parameters and take a step in the direction that most decreases the loss. If $\theta$ denotes the current parameters and $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ the loss, then a single update step is given by:
\begin{equation}`
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha > 0$ is a hyperparameter called \emph{learning rate}, that controls the step size. 

The choice of the learning rate is one of the most critical hyperparameters for the successful training of neural networks. A learning rate that is too high can cause the optimization to diverge, while a learning rate that is too small can result in very slow convergence or getting stuck in suboptimal regions. In practice, it is beneficial not only to choose a good initial learning rate but also to adjust it during training. This approach is known as \emph{learning rate scheduling} or \emph{learning rate decay} and we discuss it further in \ref{subsec:decay-scheduling}.

Computing the gradient over the entire dataset can be very slow, if not impossible due to high memory requirements. Instead, we use an approximation based on randomly selected subsets of data. This leads to two widely used variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)]: The gradient is computed using a single randomly selected data point. While this is very efficient per update, the variance of the updates is high, which can make training unstable.
    \item[Mini-batch gradient descent]: A compromise between full-batch and stochastic methods. The gradient is computed over a small batch of data points, typically ranging from 16 to 512 samples. This approach is the standard in most modern applications, offering both stability and computational efficiency.
\end{itemize}
It it well established that the mini-batch gradient descent is the way to go if applicable, and the most popular optimization algorithms used in ML are its different extensions.

\subsubsection{Optimizers}

While mini-batch gradient descent is a powerful and general optimization method, several enhancements have been developed to address its practical shortcomings, such as slow convergence, sensitivity to learning rate, and getting stuck in poorly conditioned regions of the loss landscape. These enhancements form the basis of modern optimizers used in training neural networks. In this subsection, we briefly outline the key ideas that lead to the widely used \emph{Adam} optimizer.

\paragraph{Momentum.}

One of the earliest improvements over vanilla gradient descent is the introduction of \emph{momentum}. Instead of updating parameters purely based on the current gradient, momentum accumulates an exponentially decaying moving average of past gradients. The update rule becomes:

\begin{equation} v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta) \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \label{eq:momentum_velocity} \end{equation}

\begin{equation} \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha v_t, \label{eq:momentum_update} \end{equation}

where $v_t$ is the velocity vector at iteration $t$ and $\beta \in [0,1)$ controls the contribution of previous gradients. Momentum helps accelerate updates along directions with persistent gradients and dampens oscillations in directions with high curvature.

\paragraph{Adaptive learning rates.}

Another major challenge in optimization is choosing a good learning rate. To address this, \emph{adaptive learning rate methods} adjust the learning rate individually for each parameter based on past gradients. One early method is Adagrad, which scales the learning rate inversely proportional to the square root of the sum of historical squared gradients:

\begin{equation} \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{G_{i,i} + \epsilon}} \nabla_{\theta_i} \mathcal{L}(\theta), \label{eq:adagrad_update} \end{equation}

where $G$ is the matrix accumulating the squared gradients, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant added for numerical stability. This approach makes larger updates for infrequently updated parameters and smaller updates for frequently updated ones.

However, Adagrad can suffer from aggressively shrinking learning rates. Later methods such as RMSProp address this by maintaining an exponential moving average of squared gradients instead of a cumulative sum:

\begin{equation} 
    E[g^2]t = \beta E[g^2]{t-1} + (1-\beta) (\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta))^2, 
    \label{eq:rmsprop_ema} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} 
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{E[g^2]t + \epsilon}} \nabla\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta). 
    \label{eq:rmsprop_update} 
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Adam optimizer.}

The \emph{Adam} (Adaptive Moment Estimation) optimizer combines the ideas of momentum and adaptive learning rates into a single method. It maintains two moving averages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item First moment (mean of gradients): $m_t$,
    \item Second moment (uncentered variance of gradients): $v_t$.
\end{itemize}
The updates are computed as:

\begin{equation} 
    m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), 
    \label{eq:adam_first_moment} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} 
    v_t = \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2) (\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta))^2, 
    \label{eq:adam_second_moment} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} 
    \hat{m}_t = \frac{m_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad \hat{v}_t = \frac{v_t}{1 - \beta_2^t}, 
    \label{eq:adam_bias_correction} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} 
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon}, 
    \label{eq:adam_update} 
\end{equation}

where $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are decay rates for the moment estimates, and $\epsilon$ is again a small constant for stability. The bias-corrected estimates $\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t$ account for initialization at zero.

Adam has become the optimizer of choice in many applications because of its robustness, good empirical performance, and minimal need for manual learning rate tuning. Throughout this thesis, we primarily use Adam with its default hyperparameters $(\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, \epsilon = 10^{-8})$.

\subsubsection{Decay scheduling}
\label{subsec:decay-scheduling}

\paragraph{None}
The simplest approach is to keep the learning rate constant throughout the training process. Although this is easy to implement, it usually leads to suboptimal results in more complex settings. Constant learning rate training can struggle with fine-tuning the model parameters toward convergence.

\paragraph{Linear decay}
In linear decay, the learning rate is reduced linearly from its initial value to a predefined minimum value over the course of training. It is one of the simplest decay strategies, and its intuition is clear: as training progresses, we become more conservative in updating model weights. Mathematically, if $l_0$ is the initial learning rate and $T$ is the total number of steps, then the learning rate at step $t$ is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear-decay} l(t) = l_0 \left(1 - \frac{t}{T}\right). \end{equation}

\paragraph{Exponential decay}
Exponential decay reduces the learning rate multiplicatively at each step, resulting in a faster decay than linear scheduling. It can be particularly useful when rapid early convergence is desired. The formula is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:exponential-decay} l(t) = l_0 , \gamma^{t}, \end{equation}

where $\gamma \in (0,1)$ is the decay rate per step or per epoch.

\paragraph{Cosine decay}
Cosine annealing is a decay schedule that reduces the learning rate following a cosine function, usually without any abrupt jumps. It starts at the maximum learning rate and gradually decays to the minimum value. The form is:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:cosine-decay} l(t) = l_{\mathrm{min}} + \frac{1}{2}(l_0 - l_{\mathrm{min}}) \left(1 + \cos\left( \pi \frac{t}{T} \right)\right), \end{equation}

where $l_{\mathrm{min}}$ is the final learning rate. Cosine decay is popular due to its smooth and natural shape and its empirical success in many modern applications.

\paragraph{Piecewise decay}
Piecewise decay divides the training process into several stages, within which the learning rate remains constant. After each stage, the learning rate is manually reduced, often by a factor of $10$. It can be seen as a crude approximation of an optimal decay schedule and is widely used in practice, especially when training dynamics are well-understood.

\paragraph{Plateau-based decay}
In some cases, it is preferable to adapt the learning rate dynamically based on the model's performance. \emph{Reduce on plateau} methods monitor a validation metric (e.g., validation loss) and reduce the learning rate by a factor whenever the metric stops improving for a certain number of epochs. This approach is highly adaptive and can significantly improve convergence, especially when the loss landscape is complex.

\subsubsection{Train/test split}
To evaluate how well a trained model generalizes, we need to test it on data it has never seen. The most basic method for doing this is the \emph{train/test split}. The available dataset is split into two disjoint subsets: the \emph{training set}, used to update the model parameters, and the \emph{test set}, used solely to evaluate final performance.

In some cases, an additional \emph{validation set} is used during training to tune hyperparameters or perform early stopping. This avoids leaking test set information into the training process, which would lead to overly optimistic estimates of performance.

In our experiments, we consistently use a fixed split of the dataset into training, validation, and test sets, typically in the ratio $70\% : 15\% : 15\%$. This ensures that model performance can be reliably compared across different training runs, regularization settings, and loss functions.

\subsubsection{Underfitting/overfitting}
A central challenge in training neural networks is ensuring that the model not only performs well on the training data but also generalizes to unseen examples. Two common failure modes are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item [Underfitting]: The model is too simple or not sufficiently trained and fails to capture the underlying patterns in the data. Both training and test performance are poor.
    \item[Overfitting]: The model is too complex or trained for too long and starts to memorize the training data, including noise or spurious correlations. While training performance is excellent, test performance degrades.
\end{itemize}
These phenomena can be visualized by tracking the training and validation loss over time. In underfitting, both losses remain high; in overfitting, the training loss continues to drop while the validation loss increases. Mitigating overfitting is one of the main motivations for various \emph{regularization} techniques, which are discussed in a later section.

\subsubsection{Backpropagation}
To efficiently compute gradients of the loss with respect to millions of model parameters, we use the \emph{backpropagation} algorithm. Backpropagation applies the chain rule of calculus to propagate the loss gradient from the output layer backward through the network. Each layer computes the gradient of the loss with respect to its parameters and passes the gradient with respect to its inputs to the preceding layer. This recursive process allows the entire gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ to be computed in time linear in the number of layers.

While the mathematics of backpropagation is straightforward, its implementation benefits greatly from modern automatic differentiation frameworks such as TensorFlow or PyTorch, which handle all the necessary bookkeeping and gradient computations automatically.

\section{Model architecture}
\xxx{In their essence, neural networks are function approximations and in practice we leverage them to estimate the probability distribution. Given input data $\mathbf{x}$, a neural network seeks to learn a function $f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ parameterized by weights $\theta$, such that $f_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ yields the correct or meaningful output. The particular nature of this output defines the type of problem we are solving — either supervised, unsupervised, or reinforcement learning. In our case, we use supervised learning, where each input is paired with a known label, and the goal is to learn the mapping from inputs to outputs.

\xxx{Today’s collection of neural network architectures is very diverse, with each family offering distinct trade-offs. For instance, transformer-based models excel at capturing long-range dependencies and complex structures in data, but they typically require large-scale datasets and significant computational resources — conditions that are not met in our case. Given the nature of our task and the structure of our data, the most reasonable architectural choices are \emph{fully connected networks} and \emph{convolutional neural networks (CNNs)}.}


\subsection{Fully connected network}

A fully connected neural network, often referred to as a \emph{dense network} or \emph{multilayer perceptron (MLP)}, is one of the fundamental architectures in machine learning. It consists of sequential layers where each neuron in one layer is connected to every neuron in the following layer.

The general structure is simple: the network has an \emph{input layer}, several \emph{hidden layers}, and an \emph{output layer}. Each layer transforms its input vector into an output vector through a combination of a linear transformation and a non-linear activation function.

Formally, the operation of a fully connected layer can be described as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:fully-connected} \mathbf{h} = \phi(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}), \end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the input vector, $\mathbf{W}$ is the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}$ is the bias vector, $\phi$ is a non-linear activation function (such as ReLU or sigmoid), and $\mathbf{h}$ is the resulting output (also called the hidden state).

In the context of classification, the final output layer typically applies a softmax activation function to produce a probability distribution over the possible classes.

While fully connected networks are highly expressive and can, in principle, approximate any continuous function (according to the Universal Approximation Theorem), they tend to be inefficient for structured inputs like images. This inefficiency motivated the development of specialized architectures, such as convolutional neural networks.

\subsection{Convolutional network}

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are designed to efficiently process data with a grid-like structure, most notably images. Unlike fully connected networks, CNNs leverage the spatial structure of the data by applying convolutional operations, greatly reducing the number of parameters and improving generalization.

\paragraph{Computation of convolution}

At the heart of a CNN is the \emph{convolutional layer}, where a small filter (also called a kernel) slides across the input and computes local dot products. Mathematically, a 2D convolution operation between an input $\mathbf{x}$ and a filter $\mathbf{w}$ can be expressed as:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:convolution} (\mathbf{x} * \mathbf{w})(i,j) = \sum_{m}\sum_{n} \mathbf{x}(i+m, j+n) , \mathbf{w}(m,n), \end{equation}

where $(i,j)$ are the spatial coordinates of the output, and the sums run over the spatial extent of the filter.

\paragraph{Backpropagation through convolution}

Backpropagation in convolutional layers involves calculating the gradients of the loss function with respect to the filter weights and the input. The main difference compared to fully connected layers is that weight sharing must be properly accounted for: the same filter is applied across many locations, so gradients are accumulated over all positions where the filter is used.

\paragraph{Pooling layers}

To further reduce the spatial dimensions and promote invariance to small translations, CNNs often include pooling layers. The most common variant is \emph{max pooling}, which down-samples the input by taking the maximum value over non-overlapping regions. For example, a $2\times2$ max pooling operation reduces each $2\times2$ region to a single value:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:max-pooling} y(i,j) = \max_{(m,n) \in \mathcal{R}(i,j)} x(m,n), \end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}(i,j)$ denotes the receptive field corresponding to output location $(i,j)$.

\paragraph{Depthwise separable convolutions}

Standard convolutions are computationally expensive, especially when both the input and output have many channels. One way to alleviate this is by using \emph{depthwise separable convolutions}, which decompose a convolution into two simpler operations:

\begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Depthwise convolution:} A spatial convolution is applied independently to each input channel. \item \textbf{Pointwise convolution:} A $1\times1$ convolution is applied across channels to mix the features. \end{itemize}

This decomposition significantly reduces computational cost and the number of parameters, while often maintaining competitive performance.

\paragraph{Transition to fully connected layer}

After several stages of convolutions and pooling, the high-level feature maps extracted by the CNN must be converted into a form suitable for classification. This is typically achieved by flattening the feature maps into a 1D vector and feeding it into one or more fully connected layers. These final layers map the learned feature representations to the output classes.

\subsection{Parameter-norm penalties}
A common way to regularize, used long before deeplearning, is to limit the capacity of a given model. This can be achieved by adding parameter-norm $\Omega(\bm{\theta})$ to the loss function $L(\bm{x}; \bm{\theta})$

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:loss-parameter-norm}
	\tilde{L(\bm{x}; \bm{\theta})} = L(\bm{x}; \bm{\theta}) + \alpha \Omega(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \geq 0$ is a hyperparameter weighting the contribution of the parameter-norm to the overall loss function.

This change means that will try to minize both the original loss function and some norm of the models parameters during the training. Noticeably $\Omega$ doesn't have to penalize all the parameters $\bm{\theta}$, in fact it is recommended to reagularize only proper weights $\bm{w}$ (leaving the biases $\bm{b}$ out) in this manner. While proper weights try to capture capture interactions between pairs of variables, biases only shifts the overall output. Large biases do not increase variability in activations and on the other hand having a large bias might be sometimes preferable. Therefore penalizing the biases in this way can cause significant and unnecessary underfitting.


The most common parameter norms are $L^2$:
\begin{equation}
 	\label{eq:l2-regularization}
 	\Omega(\bm{\theta}) = \dfrac{1}{2} ||\bm{w}||_2^2,
\end{equation} a
nd $L^1$:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:l1-regularization}

\subsection{Data augmentation}

The best way to improve generalization of machine learning model, is to train it on larger dataset. Getting new data is not always practical, but we can help ourselves by modifying data we already have. For our advantage, this approach is easy to apply in image classification tasks. We can rotate by 90 degrees, reflext and due to periodical boundary conditions also translate the images. These tranformations in no way change the information, and they add additional variability to our data, making it effectivelly larger. Yet this is not the biggest leverage data augmentation brings for our usecase. We can also combine images from different classes in order to help our model recognize phase transitions. There are two common approaches for such data augmentation, CutMix and MixUp. CutMix works by taking a portion of image 1 (usually rectangular segment) and pasting it onto an image 2. Resulting image will get label that is a weighted sum of original one-hot encoded labels, with weights proportional to number of pixels contributed by each image. MixUp works again by taking two images, generating random number $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ and adding pixels of these images weighted by $\alpha$ (resp. $1 - \alpha$). Label is then created in same manner.
